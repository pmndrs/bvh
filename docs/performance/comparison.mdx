## GPU performance first

For the work planned after the 0.1 release, including settings for downstream libraries and the proposed Jolt comparison, see [Runtime performance next steps](/performance/next-steps).

For a renderer, rank changes by **milliseconds on the frame's critical path**. A longer initial build can pay for itself across thousands of frames. A larger tree can also be worth keeping if it fits the device and reduces trace time. A build, upload or refit that repeats during play belongs in the frame budget.

| Workload | What the measurements mean |
| --- | --- |
| Skinning and refitting nine characters | Our pipeline used **0.60 ms CPU and 8.65 ms GPU per frame**. Three-mesh-bvh used **130.2 ms CPU** for refit. The **129.6 ms** is CPU time we **save** each frame. |
| Building Sponza once | On the i9, treating 5.74M triangles as one mesh took **3,161 ms** with our CPU tinybvh builder versus **2,045 ms** with three-mesh-bvh CENTER. The **1,116.4 ms** is extra one-time CPU build time. In a separate RTX benchmark of Sponza's 132 meshes, our H-PLOC GPU build took **46.6 ms**. End-to-end cold startup also includes preparing inputs, compiling pipelines and making the tree available to the tracer. |
| Tracing after the build | Our two-bounce tracer ran at **1.26–1.27×** three-mesh-bvh/webgpu's rate with refined tinybvh trees on the RTX test scene. The furnished room from inside is a GPU loss: **0.85×** on closest hits. No measured change closes that gap yet. |

### Runtime priorities

| Priority | Recurring cost or opportunity | Recommendation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Use today · GPU occlusion | In the RTX furnished-room test, switching from far-first to larger-first raised exterior any-hit throughput from **305 to 1,044 million rays/s** with identical visibility. That saves about **2.32 ms per million rays** at those rates. | Choose child order when creating the ray pass. See the surface-shadow and instancing results below. |
| Use today · GPU closest-hit batches | On Sponza and Bistro, the 40-byte result achieved 0.88–0.94× the compact result's RTX throughput. | Set `hitNormal: false` for consumers that never read the normal or material. This saves **6–12% of that pass's time** at the measured rates. Use the full record when shading needs those fields. |
| Use today · static geometry | Merging static instances raised RTX closest-hit throughput **1.44–1.67× on Sponza** and **1.19–1.20× on Bistro**. | Mark genuinely fixed instances `static: true` to use the existing default merge. Visibility agreed. Nearly coincident hits can select a different triangle or instance. |
| First engineering target · animated scenes | The nine-character pose and refit used **8.65 ms of GPU time per frame**, about **52%** of a 60 Hz frame and more than an entire 120 Hz frame. The published timing combines posing and tree updates. | Time those stages separately. If preparing leaf bounds dominates, one candidate is to have the leaf refit compute bounds from triangle records directly, eliminating the intermediate per-triangle bounds write and read. Compare total pose, refit **and subsequent trace** time on the same poses before adopting it. |
| Next traversal target · closest hits from within the room bounds | On RTX, three-mesh-bvh/webgpu saves **1.05 ms per million** on these random rays, or about **0.10 ms at 100k**. The shipped tie change saved no node visits on this set. | Prioritize this gap when surface bounces or camera rays in a target renderer show a corresponding frame cost. The cause is still open. Keep the measured two-bounce lead as a check on shared traversal changes. |

Two other measurements matter when their work repeats. **Moving objects queried on the CPU** cost us 239 ms more per 100k-object update than ObjectBVH on the i9. Our CPU tree rebuilds on transform edits. Refitting bounds while object membership stays stable is the first CPU change I would prioritize for that workload. **Regenerating an SDF** on the RTX 100k-triangle knot at 128³ took 103.5 ms with default signing and 81.3 ms with `sign: 'first-hit'` in the breakdown run. That option saves 22.2 ms per regeneration for a closed, consistently outward-wound, non-overlapping mesh. An SDF baked once incurs that cost only once.

**How to choose an occlusion order.** “Inside” and “outside” describe the *generated rays*. One set starts uniformly in the inner 80% of the scene bounds and points in random directions. The other starts on a surrounding sphere and aims at random points in the bounds. Neither distribution represents a camera frame or surface-to-light shadow segments. A camera inside a room can issue several different ray passes in one frame.

| Any-hit pass | Measured choice | Where it applies |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rays entering the furnished room from the surrounding sphere | `anyHitOrder: 'larger'` | On RTX it reached 1.21× three-mesh-bvh/webgpu. On rays from random interior points it also improved our rate, from 358 to 390 million rays/s, with the competitor still ahead at 413. |
| Surface shadows through merged static geometry | Keep the default `'far'` | Larger-first delivered only 0.80–0.87× far-first's throughput on RTX Sponza and Bistro. M5 also favoured far-first on merged surface rays. |
| Surface or air rays through instanced geometry | Prefer `'larger'` for passes like the measured ones | Larger-first delivered 1.11–1.48× far-first's throughput on the RTX Sponza and Bistro runs. Even with the same scene and ray origins, merging can change the best order. |

These settings change which child an **any-hit** query visits first. Every tested order returned the same visibility in the paired order checks. **Closest-hit** uses a separate traversal rule. Its shipped tie break helped the RTX exterior case from 101 to 445–449 million rays/s and left interior visit counts unchanged. Keep it. For a mixed renderer, choose defaults using GPU time for the actual mix of primary, bounce and shadow passes. A camera crossing a room boundary needs no mode switch. [Ray generator](https://github.com/pmndrs/bvh/blob/main/compare/core/rays.ts) · [Paired traversal results](/performance/builds-and-tracing)

**Why bvh.js starts faster.** On the i9, the CPU scene build took **150 ms** for bvh.js versus **414 ms** for ours at 100k objects. Both compute world boxes. Its `HybridBuilder` splits at the midpoint of the widest range of object centres, partitions the objects in place, and stores an object ID at each leaf. Our timed path builds the shared triangle tree once, inverts and packs each instance transform, and searches 16 SAH bins per axis for top-level splits. Its result can identify candidate objects. Ours can return the actual triangle hit. The benchmark times the entire build, so it does not assign the 264 ms difference to any one step. In the same 100k scene, our box queries ran at **38.4k/s** versus **19.3k/s** for bvh.js. [Scene adapters](https://github.com/pmndrs/bvh/blob/main/compare/adapters/scene.ts) · [CPU scene tables](https://github.com/pmndrs/bvh/blob/main/bench/results/compare-windows.md)

### What the extra memory buys

| Sponza, beyond the app's geometry | Our layout | three-mesh-bvh | Tradeoff |
| --- | ---: | ---: | --- |
| CPU mesh tree | 459.7 MiB with records, 284.4 MiB with shared positions | 50.6 MiB with SAH | We keep original triangle IDs and copy corners in records mode. They reuse positions and reorder the index. Our roughly two triangles per leaf versus their seven also mean more boxes to store and refit. |
| GPU trace buffers, default layouts in the RTX comparison | 355.5 MiB | 214.3 MiB | Our GPU footprint is **66% larger** here. The competitor's GPU walk also needs its index and a GPU copy of positions. |
| Our GPU layout with indexed leaves | 225.5 MiB in the Sponza layout accounting | 214.3 MiB in the GPU comparison above | Indexing saves about **130 MiB**. The measured RTX closest-hit rate on quantized nodes was **5–17% lower** with indexed `vec4` positions on the surface-ray benchmarks. Indexed and record layouts agreed on the tested hits. |

Of our default Sponza GPU layout, **263 MiB (74%) is triangle records**. Each 48-byte record carries its corners together, so a ray can test a triangle without fetching three shared vertices by index. About **92.6 MiB** is quantized nodes. The main choice here is memory versus access speed. Indexed leaves retain the geometry's precision. A faster build heuristic or larger leaves can change the amount of traversal work while preserving the geometric hit test. An SDF sign shortcut can change the actual answer for some meshes. ## Where other libraries win

**three-mesh-bvh wins CPU raycasts on the Chrome knot, CPU mesh refits, and GPU rays starting inside the furnished room on the RTX 3060. Rapier wins some CPU closest-hit workloads. bvh.js builds the fastest object tree in the scene suite.** The table gives representative losses. The [wins for @pmndrs/bvh](#where-pmndrsbvh-wins) and [tables for each scene and device](#winners-by-scene-and-device) follow.

**How to read a row.** Start with the situation and winner. The two difference columns say how much the winner saves against the slower library. For example, on an i9 Sponza refit, our default takes 595 ms and three-mesh-bvh SAH takes 231 ms. Its saving is **364 ms**, or **61% less time**. Ray and query rows convert throughput to time for the stated number of queries. Percentages for memory and tree quality instead mean fewer bytes or lower SAH cost, so milliseconds are **N/A**.

Unless a setting is named, the comparison uses our measured default against the fastest eligible external library in that test. An **unranked** result failed an agreement check. Small leads are measured margins on the named device. **Effort** is an engineering estimate for the proposed action. **Config** means a shipped option, **Low** a focused measurement or small change, **Medium** a query or builder change, and **High** a layout or update algorithm affecting several paths.

**How we could beat it** names a shipped setting, a proposed implementation change, or an open gap. A proposed change has an unmeasured gain unless the row gives a before/after result. For example, CPU closest-hit already finds one hit. Its wrapper puts the hit in an array, maps that array to public hit objects and reads element zero. Returning the same public hit directly could save allocations while preserving the triangle, point and distance. Profiling in the recommendation column would establish how much of the measured deficit those allocations explain.

## When to trade accuracy for speed

Keep the robust default when the library cannot know what an application needs. If a faster path gives the same answer for all supported inputs, it can become the default once it measures faster across the target workloads. When it can change an answer, expose the choice at the query or build step where the caller knows which errors are acceptable. A useful option names the guarantee being relaxed and gives the caller a way to estimate the gain.

| Workload | What the default protects | When the faster choice fits | Status and evidence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Signed distance field | Parity voting tolerates inconsistent triangle winding on a closed surface. | With closed, outward-wound, non-overlapping shells, use `sign: 'first-hit'`. Outside those conditions a voxel can get the wrong sign even when its distance magnitude is accurate. | **Available.** On the RTX 100k knot at 128³, the field took 103.5 ms with parity and 81.3 ms with first-hit in the same breakdown run. |
| CPU nearest-point query | The default searches for the nearest point, even after finding one close to the query. | A proximity trigger only needs a witness inside its activation radius. Pass that radius as `minThreshold` to `closestPointToPoint` to stop on the first qualifying candidate. Its point, distance and triangle can differ from the true nearest. | **Available per query.** Savings depend on how early a candidate qualifies. The comparison suite has no paired timing for this option. |
| GPU closest point on an identity-transformed instance | The general instance path preserves its box padding and floating-point behaviour at boundaries. | A distance-only consumer may accept either triangle on a tie. Picking or material lookup may care which one wins. The prototype can also change a touching shape test or the sign of zero. | **Measured candidate.** Reading the instance as merged was about 18% faster on the RTX closest-point workload. The knot's distance field was byte-identical. An independent query option for instances would let callers accept those edge cases. |
| CPU closest point on very thin triangles | The fallback checks triangle edges when the ordinary closest-point calculation loses precision. Without it, the sliver sweep found distances up to 1.7 units too far on a unit triangle. | A mesh pipeline that rules out slivers, including after deformation, could skip the guard. Another caller might explicitly accept that error for approximate queries. | **Candidate.** The protection cost about 2–5% in the recorded CPU query measurements. Benchmark a tree built with this assumption against the robust path on typical meshes and report the error separately on slivers. |

[Signed distance fields](/tracing/sdf) · [Closest-point thresholds](/cpu-queries/closest-point) · [Identity-transform experiment, section 12](https://github.com/pmndrs/bvh/blob/main/docs/internals/WINDOWS-RUNBOOK.md) · [CPU sliver measurements](https://github.com/pmndrs/bvh/blob/main/compare/RESULTS.md)

Some workloads can skip work while preserving every answer they consume. A GPU closest-hit batch can omit unused normal and material fields with `hitNormal: false` (24 bytes per result, down from 40). Any-hit child order preserves visibility and changes how quickly a blocker is found. The batch comparison already disables hit normals and masks, so neither explains its remaining closest-hit deficit. [Batch layout and timings](/tracing/batches-and-the-cpu-walk) · [Traversal orders](/tracing/wgsl-walk)

### Adding Jolt Physics

Jolt is an unmeasured candidate for **CPU collision queries and batched rays**. Its browser distribution includes a multithreaded WASM build with SIMD enabled by default. Its public ray binding accepts one ray per call. A loop calling it from JavaScript measures that API's throughput. Demonstrating scaling across cores requires dispatching independent rays to workers. [Jolt browser builds](https://github.com/jrouwe/JoltPhysics.js#readme) · [Jolt query bindings](https://github.com/jrouwe/JoltPhysics.js/blob/main/JoltJS.idl)

| Comparison | Implementation | Effort estimate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Browser CPU picking and all hits | Add Jolt as a comparison-only dependency. Build a mesh shape from the same indexed triangles, store original triangle IDs as per-triangle user data, and enable backface hits. Give each Jolt ray a segment long enough to pass the scene bounds and convert hit fractions back to scene distances. Use the existing seeded rays and agreement checks. Report warm latency and throughput separately from module load and tree build. | 1–2 engineering days |
| Browser CPU ray batches across cores | Add a batch entry point that passes packed rays into WASM once, partitions them into jobs over one read-only tree and writes disjoint results. Run the same batch implementation with one and several workers, holding SIMD fixed. Serve the threaded page with cross-origin isolation. Report worker count, batch size, elapsed time to usable results, and main-thread occupancy. | About 3–5 additional days for a native binding, browser harness and verification |

For CPU scaling, give both implementations the same worker budget and distinguish a single pick's latency from sustained batch throughput. For GPU comparisons, specify where the rays originate and where the next consumer needs the answers. CPU picking includes GPU submission and readback on our WebGPU path. A GPU lighting pass using Jolt would also need to get its rays to the CPU and upload results. Time the relevant transfers on both paths and report the warm batch size where one overtakes the other, if it does. Jolt has no measured winner or loser rows here yet. ### Method: `compare/skinned`

Nine skinned characters (tubes on a chain of six bones, `?characters=`, `?rings=`). In each measured frame both
libraries see the same pose (the bones are set from the frame's time), in alternating order:

- **three-mesh-bvh** (its `skinnedMesh` example): one `SkinnedMeshBVH` per character and `refit()` each frame, which
  skins every vertex on the CPU (`SkinnedMesh.getVertexPosition`). CPU ms is the refits' wall time. Its trees stay on
  the CPU; to trace the pose on the GPU it re-packs them with `BVHComputeData.update()`, which skins every vertex
  again. That is a row of its own (the upload itself happens at the next compute and is not counted).
- **ours**: `SceneTreesObject3DSync` with the renderer poses each `SkinnedMesh` on the GPU (`BvhPosePass`) and
  `SceneTrees.setPose` refits its tree from that buffer, in the frame's encoder. CPU ms is the sync and the recording;
  GPU ms is the pose passes (three's compute timestamps) plus what `SceneTrees.update` records (its
  `timestampWrites`), or without `timestamp-query` the wall time to the queue being done (an upper bound).

Medians over the measured frames. Tree quality is reported beside the times: three-mesh-bvh's summed SAH score against
its fresh build (`getBVHExtremes`), and our refit and drift-rebuild counts. The check: on a frozen pose, a top-down grid
of rays through our GPU-refit trees agrees with three-mesh-bvh's refit trees (CPU raycast) on every ray away from a
silhouette.

### Method: `compare/pathtracer`

One kernel (`examples/three/pathtracer/kernel.ts`: jittered camera rays, PCG random numbers, diffuse bounces, one sun
shadow ray per hit, accumulation) over two walks: ours through `BvhTraceNodes` (`traceClosest`, `traceAny`, the
library's defaults: merged static meshes, quantized nodes), and three-mesh-bvh's `BVHComputeData` (a `MeshBVH` with SAH
per geometry) through `bvh_RaycastFirstHit` and `bvh_RaycastAnyHit` in a `wgslFn`. Both read one palette colour per
mesh, so the shading is the same. Measured in paired rounds (the two back to back, the order reversed every other
round, the ratio taken within each round):

- **path**: every ray of the full tracer, closest and any hit;
- **closest**: the tracer with its shadow rays compiled out (the same closest-hit rays, since the shadow ray draws no
  random number);
- **any**: the shadow rays alone, derived as their count over the time the full tracer takes beyond the closest-only
  one (noisier than the other two).

Rays are counted in the kernel (one atomic per pixel); time is three's compute timestamps, else the wall time of one
dispatch with the queue drained before and after. Each pixel's first hit is checked against ours; a pixel through a
silhouette may graze either way between two different triangle tests, so up to 0.1% of pixels may differ.
